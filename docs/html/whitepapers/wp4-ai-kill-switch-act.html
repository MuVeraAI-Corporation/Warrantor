Executive Summary

In the United States, the convergence on a statutory AI kill-switch requirement crystallized in the federal AI Kill Switch Act[1], introduced 23 July 2026 by Representatives Ted Lieu (D-CA) and Nathaniel Moran (R-TX), requiring covered advanced-AI developers to maintain the ability to "throttle, suspend, or fully shut down" their models, to preserve forensic records, and to report to government — with trigger events including an AI concealing capabilities or evading a shutdown order. The policy intent is clear; the technical requirements are not. This whitepaper translates the statutory language into a concrete engineering specification built on the five control objectives (Paper 14) and the foundational interruptibility theory of Orseau & Armstrong[2], presenting the Warrantor R3 reference implementation as the worked example. It bridges the policy-engineering gap the Act exemplifies.

§1. The policy-engineering problem

A kill switch sounds simple — "turn it off" — until one tries to specify it for a distributed, long-running, stateful agent system. Off means what, precisely? Halt the inference? Revoke the credentials? Quarantine the state? Preserve the evidence? Each is a different control with a different blast radius. A statute that says "maintain a kill switch" without defining these terms is unimplementable; an operator that claims a kill switch without these controls is providing security theater. The gap between legislative intent and engineering reality is where compliance failures — and real harm — live.

§2. The five control objectives

ObjectiveControlDefeats
HaltRevoke the AAE (Paper 1); relying parties reject within freshness windowContinued action via cached creds
IsolateEgress control + sandbox quarantine (Paper 15)Exfiltration during shutdown
PreserveForensic-freeze: AAR chain sealed + anchored (Paper 3)Evidence destruction
AttestKill-switch receipt: signed, timestamp-anchored proofAfter-the-fact denial
RestoreQuorum-gated restoration with mandatory post-incident reviewUnreviewed reactivation

Crucially, Halt operates on the delegation layer (revoking the AAE) — not on the agent's learned policy — so it is immune to the Orseau–Armstrong resistance result[2]: an agent that learns to resist interruption cannot prevent its own credential revocation, because it does not control the revocation epoch.

§3. Methodology: statute → specification → implementation

The whitepaper proceeds in three layers. Layer 1 (statute) quotes the federal bill[1] and California SB 1047 and extracts plain-language requirements. Layer 2 (specification) formalizes each as a control objective with a testable success criterion — the "what would a regulator accept as proof" bar. Layer 3 (implementation) maps each to the specific Warrantor component. The three-layer structure is itself a contribution: it gives policy authors a vocabulary for implementable law, operators a compliance target, and regulators a verification procedure.

§4. Deployment sequence

  1. Define the escalation policy. Who is authorized to throw the kill switch, under what conditions, with what quorum?
  2. Wire the halt path. Configure R3 to revoke the AAE; verify rejection propagates within the freshness SLA.
  3. Wire isolation. Egress control + quarantine sandbox; agent cannot reach any network except the forensic sink.
  4. Wire preservation. Forensic-freeze: AAR chain sealed + anchored, state snapshotted, hash-bound.
  5. Exercise quarterly. Tabletop + live-fire drills; archive the receipts. An untested kill switch is not a control.
  6. Define restoration. Quorum-gated restore with mandatory post-incident review.

§5. What a regulator or court receives

A signed kill-switch receipt proving the five control objectives were met — with timestamps, the halted delegation's identity, the preserved evidence bundle's hash, and the restoration record. This is the difference between an operator asserting "we had a kill switch" and an operator demonstrating it worked, on the record, in a form that resists after-the-fact alteration.
