Abstract

The EU AI Act's data-governance (Article 10) and transparency obligations require, for high-risk AI, documented bias auditing and training-data provenance that supports copyright compliance — obligations that are today satisfied, when at all, by hand-authored reports of uncertain integrity. We present an integrated bias-and-copyright audit pipeline built on three verified foundations: the data-provenance framework of Longpre et al.[1] (ICML 2024 Spotlight), the NIST Generative AI Profile's twelve risk areas[2], and the NIST synthetic-content-detection technical report[3] on watermarking and content credentials. The pipeline automatically binds bias-audit results and dataset-copyright provenance to the model via the lineage ledger (Paper 10) and the AIBOM (Paper 11), producing the evidence the Act demands as a by-product of operation rather than a retroactive report. We argue that auditable provenance is the substrate that makes both bias and copyright compliance demonstrable rather than asserted.

§1. Introduction: two obligations, one substrate

EU AI Act Article 10 demands data governance — including bias examination — for high-risk AI; the transparency articles demand that generated content be watermarkable and that training-data copyright be respected. These are usually treated as two separate compliance burdens. They share a substrate: both require provable provenance over the data-to-model path. A bias audit is only credible if the data it audited is provably the data the model trained on; a copyright defense is only credible if the dataset's licensing provenance is provably what the model consumed. The lineage ledger (Paper 10) is that substrate; this paper builds the two audit components on top of it.

§2. Background: provenance, GenAI risk, watermarking

Longpre et al.[1] (ICML 2024 Spotlight, "A Framework for Data Provenance for AI") evaluates current provenance tools and outlines universal provenance standards for foundation-model training data — directly mapping to Article 10's data-governance demand. The NIST Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1)[2] defines twelve risk areas including content provenance, watermarking, and bias — the authoritative US-government risk taxonomy. The NIST AI 100-4 report[3] details digital watermarking, metadata recording, and synthetic-content authentication (operationalizing C2PA-style credentials) for the transparency articles. The EPRS briefing[4] provides the EU-institutional analysis of watermarking under the Act.

§3. The bias-audit component

The bias audit (A3) runs the model against a benchmark suite stratified by protected attributes, producing per-stratum performance metrics and disparity measurements. The audit's credibility rests on its binding to the data: the audit references the dataset manifest hash from the lineage ledger (Paper 10), so the result is provably about the data the model actually consumed. The result is recorded as a lineage entry, becoming part of the model's auditable history.

§4. The copyright-provenance component

The copyright component tracks, per dataset shard, the licensing provenance (the Longpre framework's provenance records[1]), and gates consumption: a shard whose license conflicts with the deployment context (e.g., a non-commercial license in a commercial deployment) is refused at training admission. The provenance is bound into the AIBOM (Paper 11) and the lineage ledger, so a copyright defense is demonstrable — the operator can show which shards were consumed under which licenses, with the integrity guarantee the ledger provides.

§5. Binding audits to the model

Both audit results are bound to the model via the Safetensors++ provenance block (Paper 9) and recorded in the lineage ledger (Paper 10). The composition means a regulator asking "was this model bias-audited, and on what data?" receives the audit result and the proof the audit was over the consumed data — in one verifiable graph walk, not two separate reports of uncertain linkage.

§8. Conclusion

Bias and copyright obligations share a substrate — provable data provenance — which the lineage ledger provides. Building the two audit components on it converts the obligations from hand-authored reports into operationally-produced, lineage-bound evidence. Auditable provenance is what makes both demonstrable rather than asserted.
