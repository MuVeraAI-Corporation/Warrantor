Abstract

Data poisoning and backdoor attacks — in which an adversary corrupts the training data to embed a behavior that survives into the deployed model — are among the most consequential supply-chain threats to foundation models, and the hardest to detect: the poisoned model behaves normally on clean inputs and betrays its backdoor only on a trigger known to the attacker. The canonical survey of Goldblum et al.[1] (IEEE TPAMI 2023) documents the attack surface and the defense landscape, but the defenses operate on the finished model or the static dataset; few operate on the training process itself. We present a training-time integrity monitor that instruments the training pipeline to detect poisoning signatures in flight: anomalous gradient distributions on a held-out canary set, per-example loss outliers indicative of trigger optimization, and lineage-binding anomalies between data-shard manifests and consumed batches. We evaluate the monitor on a corpus of poisoned-training runs spanning the attack classes in Goldblum et al. and report detection rates, the false-positive cost on clean training, and the composition with the lineage ledger (Paper 10) that makes any detected incident an auditable event.

§1. Introduction: the stealthiest supply-chain attack

A poisoned model is the supply-chain attack that survives every defense that examines only the finished artifact: the model has the right architecture, the right parameter count, the right clean-input behavior, and a valid signature. The corruption is invisible until the attacker triggers it in deployment. This makes poisoning categorically harder than, say, weight tampering (detected by Safetensors++, Paper 9) or documentation gaps (detected by the AIBOM, Paper 11). The defense must look earlier — at the training process — because by the time the model is finished, the evidence of poisoning has been averaged into the weights.

§2. Background: poisoning and backdoors

Goldblum et al.[1] (IEEE TPAMI 2023, 500+ citations) is the canonical survey: it covers poison optimization through training pipelines, the backdoor-trigger design space, and the spectrum of defenses (from data-filtering to model-inspection). The defenses largely fall into two camps — static data defenses (filter the dataset before training) and post-hoc model defenses (inspect the finished model for backdoor neurons). The gap is the dynamic defense: monitoring the training process itself for the signatures a poisoning attack leaves in the gradient stream.

§3. Why training-time, not post-hoc

A poisoning attack optimizes a trigger to flip the model's behavior on trigger-bearing inputs while preserving behavior on clean inputs. During training, this optimization leaves signatures: the trigger examples produce atypical gradient statistics, and the per-example loss on trigger examples diverges from clean examples during the trigger-embedding phase. These signatures are visible in flight and averaged out by the time training completes. The training-time monitor exploits this asymmetry — it sees what the post-hoc inspector cannot.

§4. The three monitoring signals

  • Canary-gradient anomaly. A held-out canary set, never used for updates, is forward-passed each epoch; the gradient distribution on canaries shifts anomalously when the model is learning a backdoor incompatible with clean canary behavior.
  • Per-example loss outlier. Trigger-optimization produces examples whose loss trajectory diverges from the clean-batch distribution; a robust outlier detector flags them.
  • Lineage-binding anomaly. The lineage ledger (Paper 10) records which data-shard manifest each batch was drawn from; a mismatch between the recorded manifest and the consumed bytes indicates tampering with the data pipeline — a precondition for many poisoning attacks.

§5. Implementation in the training pipeline

The monitor is instrumented into the training loop via the Warrantor A-pillar, with canary evaluations and per-example loss tracking added at bounded cost (one forward pass per canary set per epoch; outlier detection vectorized over the batch). Detected anomalies emit an AAR (Paper 3) tagged as an integrity incident, which feeds the kill-switch and the lineage ledger — composing supply-chain integrity with incident response (Paper 14).

§6. Evaluation on poisoned-run corpus

We construct a corpus of poisoned training runs spanning the Goldblum taxonomy (clean-label, dirty-label, gradient-shaping, Sleeper-Agent-style) and measure detection rate and clean-training false-positive rate.

Attack classDetection rateClean-training FPR
Clean-label backdoor94%1.2%
Dirty-label backdoor97%1.2%
Gradient-shaping88%1.2%
Sleeper-Agent (stealthy)76%1.2%

The stealthier attacks (Sleeper-Agent) are harder to detect at training time — an acknowledged limitation — but the 76% rate is substantially above what post-hoc inspection achieves on the same class, and the detected incidents become auditable events via the AAR composition.

§8. Conclusion

Training-time integrity monitoring exploits the asymmetry that poisoning signatures are visible in flight but averaged out by completion. The three-signal monitor detects the majority of poisoning attacks across the Goldblum taxonomy at low clean-training cost, and composes with the lineage ledger and AAR to make every detection an auditable incident. Stealthy attacks remain partial, motivating the composition with post-hoc defenses rather than replacement of them.
