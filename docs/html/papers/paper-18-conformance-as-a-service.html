Abstract

Project Wycheproof[1] — Google's collection of cryptographic test vectors that detect known weaknesses across implementations — established that conformance test vectors are a first-class security artifact, not an afterthought. The differential-fuzzing tradition[2] extends this by feeding random inputs across implementations and comparing outputs. We argue that AI-agent security protocols (the AAE of Paper 1, the AAR of Paper 3) require the same treatment: a Conformance-as-a-Service offering that runs a maintained corpus of cross-language conformance vectors against any claimed-conformant implementation and produces a verifiable conformance report. We present the design, the vector corpus (V2/V3), and the differential-Dolev-Yao fuzzing extension[3] that detects protocol vulnerabilities requiring symbolic-attacker reasoning. The service is the structural mechanism that makes an AumOS "compatible" claim verifiable rather than asserted.

§1. Introduction: from assertion to verification

An implementer who claims "AumOS-compatible" or "AAE-conformant" makes an assertion. The relying party who needs to trust the claim has, today, no neutral way to verify it — they must either trust the implementer or reverse-engineer the test. This is the gap that conformance-as-a-service closes: a maintained, runnable corpus of vectors that any implementation can be tested against, producing a report a relying party can verify. The Wycheproof project[1] proved the model for cryptographic primitives; we extend it to AI-agent protocols.

§2. Background: Wycheproof and differential fuzzing

Wycheproof[1] (originally Google, now community-maintained by C2SP) is a format-agnostic JSON collection of test vectors that detect known cryptographic weaknesses (invalid-curve, bias, truncation) across implementations in Java, Go, Rust, C, Python, JS. The Quarkslab differential-fuzzing survey[2] catalogs tools (Cryptofuzz/CDF) that complement Wycheproof by generating random inputs and comparing outputs across implementations. The recent differential-Dolev-Yao fuzzing work[3] detects protocol vulnerabilities that require symbolic-attacker reasoning — beyond what state-of-the-art fuzzers catch. These three are the lineage; the service unifies and specializes them to agent protocols.

§3. Conformance-as-a-Service design

The service accepts an implementation-under-test (a binary or container exposing the protocol's verification API), runs the maintained vector corpus against it across the relevant runtimes, and produces a signed conformance report: pass/fail per vector, divergence-class per failure, and an overall conformance verdict. The report is itself an in-toto attestation (Paper 11 lineage), so a relying party can verify it was produced by the genuine service over the genuine corpus.

§4. The vector corpus

The V2/V3 corpus covers the AAE (Paper 1) and AAR (Paper 3) protocols: golden canonical-encoding vectors (Paper 2's CSF-v1 conformance), delegation-chain vectors (Paper 4's intersection), and tamper-detection vectors (Paper 3's operator-adversary harness). Each vector is documented with the defect class it targets and the expected verdict.

§5. The Dolev-Yao fuzzing extension

Beyond the fixed corpus, the service runs differential-Dolev-Yao fuzzing[3]: it generates arbitrary protocol message sequences under a Dolev-Yao attacker model and compares the verdicts across runtimes, catching vulnerabilities that exploit the protocol's symbolic-attacker surface rather than its encoding. This is the layer that finds novel bugs, where the corpus finds known ones.

§8. Conclusion

Conformance-as-a-Service makes the "AumOS-compatible" claim verifiable rather than asserted, extending the Wycheproof model to agent protocols and adding differential Dolev-Yao fuzzing for novel-vulnerability discovery. It is the structural mechanism that underwrites the neutrality guarantee of the open defense stack.
