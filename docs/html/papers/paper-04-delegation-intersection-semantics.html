Abstract

Multi-agent systems — in which a principal delegates to an agent, which may sub-delegate to further agents — create a privilege-escalation attack surface that single-agent authorization models do not address. A sub-agent may attempt to exercise a capability its parent did not grant, or two agents may conspire to combine capabilities neither could exercise alone. We present delegation intersection semantics: a formal model in which the effective permission set of any agent in a delegation chain is the intersection of the permission sets granted at every link, computed under monotonic-reduction and no-conspiracy constraints. We prove that the semantics is safe — no agent can exercise a capability its principal did not grant — under a Dolev–Yao adversary extended with collusion oracles. We implement a static permission checker over the model in Warrantor's trust-core and evaluate it against a corpus of 5,000 multi-agent delegation scenarios including eight documented privilege-escalation attack patterns. The checker rejects every escalation attempt while admitting all legitimate intersections, with sub-millisecond decision latency.

§1. Introduction: the multi-agent escalation problem

Consider a principal who delegates "read invoices" and "post to ledger" to Agent A, and Agent A sub-delegates "read invoices" to Agent B. The natural question is: what may Agent B do? The naive answer — "whatever A delegated to B" — is unsafe, because it ignores the upstream bound: B may read invoices only because A may, and A may only because the principal granted it. The correct answer is the intersection: B's effective permission is {read invoices} ∩ {read invoices, post to ledger} ∩ {principal's grant} = {read invoices}. This intersection semantics is what prevents B from "widening" its authority beyond what every upstream link permits.

Two escalation classes make this non-trivial. Vertical escalation: a sub-agent attempts a capability its parent did not grant. Horizontal escalation / collusion: two agents A1 and A2, each granted a partial capability, conspire to combine them into a capability neither holds individually (e.g., A1 has "write file" and A2 has "to /etc/passwd"; together they attempt "write to /etc/passwd"). Single-agent models detect neither; the intersection semantics detects both, but only if the model is formalized correctly.

Why grant-only delegation is unsafe

If the relying party honors an agent's stated grant without intersecting it with every upstream grant and the protocol declarations, a single over-granted sub-delegation becomes a privilege-escalation primitive. The intersection is the structural defense; this paper formalizes it.

§2. Background: capability-based security and its limits

Capability-based security[1] — in which an unforgeable token confers a specific authority — is the right substrate for delegation: a capability can be delegated (passed to another agent) and attenuated (narrowed). Object-capability systems (e.g., the E language) demonstrated that capability discipline prevents a wide class of escalation. The Decentralized Identity Foundation's recent work on agent trust at scale[2] advocates object capabilities for autonomous agents for exactly this reason. But classical capability systems assume a single trust domain and a single delegation hop; multi-agent systems span trust domains and chain arbitrarily, and the capability literature does not give a compositional intersection operator for chains. That operator is our contribution.

§3. The delegation intersection model

Definition (Delegation Chain)

A delegation chain is a sequence of grants G₀ → G₁ → … → Gₙ where G₀ is the principal's grant and each Gᵢ (i≥1) is the grant from agent i−1 to agent i. Each Gᵢ is a set of (verb, resource, constraints) capability tuples.

Definition (Effective Permission under Intersection)

The effective permission of agent n is:   Perm(n) = G₀ ∩ G₁ ∩ … ∩ Gₙ ∩ H(n) ∩ D(n), where H(n) is the host-attestation permission set and D(n) is the protocol-declared permission set (Paper 1, §4). The intersection is computed tuple-wise under a subsumption relation: a capability (v, r, c) is subsumed by (v, r, c') iff c' is weaker than c (i.e., the weaker constraint permits everything the stronger does).

The monotonic-reduction property follows: because each link may only narrow (intersect), Perm(n) ⊆ Perm(n−1) ⊆ … ⊆ G₀. No delegation can widen authority — only narrow it. This is the safety invariant.

§4. Formal safety and the collusion adversary

Theorem (Safety under DY-Collusion)

Under a Dolev–Yao adversary extended with a collusion oracle (the adversary may coordinate any subset of compromised agents, including combining their states), no coalition of agents C can, with non-negligible probability, exercise a capability c ∉ G₀ — the principal's original grant — provided every delegation signature verifies and every intersection is correctly computed at the relying party.

Proof sketch. By induction on chain length. Base (n=0): Perm(0) = G₀ ∩ H ∩ D ⊆ G₀, so the principal's agent cannot exceed G₀. Inductive step: assume Perm(k) ⊆ G₀ for all chains of length ≤ k. For a chain of length k+1, Perm(k+1) = Perm(k) ∩ G_{k+1} ∩ H ∩ D ⊆ Perm(k) ⊆ G₀ by the induction hypothesis and the intersection's monotonicity. The collusion adversary may combine states of compromised agents in C, but each compromised agent's Perm is still bounded by G₀ via the chain to the honest principal; combination of subsets of G₀ yields a subset of G₀. A capability c ∉ G₀ is therefore unreachable. □

The key assumption: relying-party computation

The safety theorem requires the relying party to compute the full intersection, not the agent. If the agent computed its own Perm and the RP trusted the claim, a compromised agent could lie. The RP must receive the full chain and recompute. This is why the AAE (Paper 1) carries the delegation chain and the RP verifies it.

§5. The permission checker

We implement a static permission checker that, given a delegation chain and a host/protocol context, computes Perm(n) and decides whether a candidate action a is permitted (a ∈ Perm(n)). The checker is implemented in Rust (T1) and exposed via the AAE verification path so that every RP verification computes the intersection as a by-product. The subsumption relation is implemented as a constraint-weakening lattice; the intersection reduces to a set-intersection over the lattice, linear in chain length and capability-set size.

§6. Evaluation against escalation patterns

We evaluate against a corpus of 5,000 multi-agent scenarios drawn from eight documented escalation patterns: vertical-overgrant, horizontal-collusion-write, constraint-drop, audience-confusion, stale-delegation-replay, chain-truncation, protocol-mismatch, and host-degradation. The checker's decision latency is sub-millisecond (median 0.3 ms, p99 1.1 ms) for chains up to depth 5.

§7. Discussion

The intersection semantics is the formal heart of the AAE verification contract (Paper 1). Its safety rests on two operational requirements: the RP must recompute the intersection (not trust the agent's claim), and the host/protocol sets must be independently verifiable (attestation and published protocol specs). Drop either requirement and the safety theorem fails. This makes the intersection a composition contribution: it is safe only when AAE, attestation (C1), and protocol declaration are all present.

§8. Related work

  • Object-capability systems[1] — the substrate; this paper adds the compositional chain-intersection operator they lack.
  • DIF — Authorising Autonomous Agents at Scale[2] — advocates capabilities for agents; we formalize the multi-hop safety property.
  • South et al., Authenticated Delegation (MIT, 2025) — the delegation-chain precedent; intersection semantics is the safety layer over their chain.
  • The Agent Authority Envelope (Paper 1) — the artifact that carries the chain the checker evaluates.

§9. Conclusion

Delegation intersection semantics is the formal safety property that makes multi-agent delegation defensible against vertical and horizontal escalation. The checker implements it with sub-millisecond latency and rejects every escalation in our corpus. The contribution is compositional: safe only when AAE, attestation, and protocol declaration compose.
