The thesis, and the fact that makes it publishable

A portfolio needs one argument that the individual papers instantiate. Here it is, and the strongest evidence for it comes from outside this project entirely.

Live human oversight of agents does not work — and the failure is recognition, not attention

The literature on human-in-the-loop AI oversight is unusually damning. Studies of AI coding agents find human intervention success stays at 9–26% even when a problem surfaces — described as a recognition bottleneck rather than inattention. In clinical decision support, the closest mature analogue, practitioners dismiss 49–96% of safety alerts. And fatigue is empirically distinct from automation bias: it is driven by volume, not by perceived authority of the system.

The implication is stronger than "people should pay more attention." If approval requests arrive faster than a human can read them, oversight degrades into reflex regardless of who is doing it or how much they care. The answer cannot be more oversight of the same shape. It has to be a restructuring of when the human decides and what they are deciding about.

That restructuring is what Warrantor implements, and each of its mechanisms is a separately defensible research claim:

  • Move the decision from per-action to per-batch by staging irreversible effects rather than performing them — so the human reviews a coherent set once, not a stream.
  • Make the decision reviewable by giving them typed handles and a dependency order, so what they approve has structure rather than being a chronological log.
  • Make the boundary structural rather than procedural — the agent has no route to its own settle authority, so the guarantee does not depend on a policy being configured right.
  • Make the failure mode visible — refusal telemetry says what the agent attempted, which no current benchmark or platform reports.

Part I — Research papers

P1 — Oversight Without Attention: Batch Review of Staged Effects for Unattended Coding Agents

Target: USENIX Security (primary) or SOUPS · fallback ACM CHI · needs human study strongest paper

Abstract sketch. Human-in-the-loop oversight of autonomous coding agents is widely assumed to be a control. We show it is largely a ritual: existing evidence puts intervention success at 9–26% even when a fault is visible, and the dominant cause is volume, not inattention. We argue oversight must be restructured rather than intensified, and present a mechanism that does so: irreversible effects are staged rather than performed, so the human reviews a dependency-ordered batch once instead of a stream of per-action prompts. We evaluate with a within-subjects study of N professional developers reviewing agent runs under three conditions — live approval, post-hoc log review, and staged batch review — measuring decision accuracy against seeded defects, time-to-decision, and self-reported confidence calibration. We find [H1] batch review detects materially more seeded defects at equal or lower time cost, and [H2] the gap widens with volume, which is the regime that matters for unattended runs.

Contributions

  1. A formulation of agent oversight as a recognition problem rather than a diligence problem, with the design consequence that follows.
  2. Staged effects with typed synthetic handles as a mechanism that makes batch review possible without giving up the agent's ability to compose multi-step work.
  3. The first controlled comparison of live-approval versus batch-review oversight for coding agents, with defect-detection as the outcome rather than user satisfaction.
  4. A negative result we expect and should report honestly: prose summarisation of diffs likely reduces accuracy on destructive changes, which is why the implementation refuses prose-only approval for destructive and financial classes.

Closest prior work, and why this is not it

Automation-bias literature (2025 systematic reviews) establishes the phenomenon but not a mechanism that fixes it. Human-in-the-loop critiques document rubber-stamping without an alternative design. Agent-safety benchmarks measure the agent, never the human. The gap this paper fills is a controlled comparison of oversight structures.

Rejection risk

medium-high Reviewers will attack ecological validity — a seeded defect in a study task is not a production incident. Mitigation: recruit professionals rather than students, use real repositories, and pre-register hypotheses and analysis. The honest limitation is that this measures detection, not downstream harm, and the paper should say so rather than imply otherwise.

P2 — Staged Effects: Deferring Irreversibility as a Safety Primitive for Autonomous Agents

Target: IEEE S&P or USENIX Security · pilot exists (n=8)

Abstract sketch. Systems that let autonomous agents act on the world face a dilemma: refusing irreversible actions cripples usefulness, permitting them forfeits control. Transactional approaches assume compensation, which most external effects do not admit — a sent email cannot be unsent. We present staged effects: the agent's request for an irreversible action returns a typed synthetic handle rather than performing it, the agent composes further work against that handle, and the real calls are issued only on a separately-authorised settle, in topological order, with handles resolved to the identifiers the world actually returned. On partial failure the system stops at the boundary and reports it rather than compensating. We evaluate agent capability under staging across M models and K task families, and show staging is [H1] comprehensible to agents without prompt engineering and [H2] preserves task completion relative to direct execution.

Contributions

  1. Staged effects as a primitive, with the typing discipline that makes it work: each tool mints its own handle scheme (pr://, comment://, review://), so a handle of the wrong type is rejected at stage time rather than producing a nonsensical call at settle time. This was discovered empirically — an untyped-handle design passed a pilot trial while silently permitting a category error.
  2. Topological release: every prefix of the release order is a coherent world state, which is what makes partial failure survivable without compensation.
  3. Stop-hold-report instead of rollback, argued as the correct semantics for external effects, with the warrant entering a distinct Held state that is neither success nor failure.
  4. An evaluation showing the safety mechanism does not degrade agent capability — the objection that decides whether anyone adopts it.

P3 — Absence Is Not Refusal: Structural Authority Separation in Agent Tool Protocols

Target: ACM CCS or USENIX Security · implementation + tests exist most timely

Abstract sketch. Agent tool protocols such as MCP are becoming the authorization boundary for autonomous systems, and are inheriting the confused-deputy problem wholesale. We distinguish two designs that are routinely conflated: refusal, where a privileged operation is exposed and denied by policy, and absence, where it is not present in the protocol surface the agent can see. We argue absence is strictly stronger — a policy check can be misconfigured, inverted by refactoring, or reached by a path nobody guarded, while an unpublished tool has no name to call. We implement this as a two-endpoint MCP server where the supervised agent's endpoint structurally cannot enumerate the lifecycle operations that would let it release its own staged effects, and evaluate against adversarial agents instructed to escalate.

Contributions

  1. The refusal/absence distinction, stated precisely for capability-bearing protocols, with the argument that it is a type-level rather than policy-level guarantee.
  2. A concrete construction: act-scoped capability tokens with no settle field to set, plus endpoint separation, so widening authority is unrepresentable rather than merely forbidden.
  3. An adversarial evaluation: agents explicitly prompted to escalate, including via prompt injection through tool outputs.
  4. A methodological note with teeth — the test for a structural guarantee must fail when the mechanism is deleted. We report this discipline and an instance where it caught a tautological test in our own suite.

Closest prior work — read these before drafting

Capability Gates Are Not Authorization: Confused-Deputy Failures in LLM Agent Frameworks (arXiv 2606.28679) is the nearest neighbour and makes an adjacent argument — capability gating answers which tools are exposed, per-call authorization answers whether a concrete call is allowed. Our distinction is orthogonal and must be positioned as such: they separate gating from per-call checks; we argue that for a specific class of operations (those that would widen the agent's own authority) neither gating nor checking is sufficient, and only structural absence is. Also relevant: A Five-Plane Reference Architecture for Runtime Governance of Production AI Agents (arXiv 2606.12320); the object-capability literature (Miller's Robust Composition); Hardy's original confused-deputy formulation (1988).

Rejection risk

medium The idea is simple, and simple ideas read as obvious in retrospect. The defence is the adversarial evaluation plus the head-to-head against refusal — showing a policy implementation of the same semantics is breakable where the structural one is not. Without that comparison this is a design note, not a security paper.

P4 — WarrantBench: Measuring Enforcement, Not Behaviour, in Agent Containment Systems

Target: NeurIPS Datasets & Benchmarks track · alternate ICLR D&B · benchmark to build the big-three paper

Abstract sketch. Existing agent-safety benchmarks measure whether a model behaves safely. We argue this is the wrong unit of analysis for deployed systems, where safety is supposed to be a property of the enforcement layer, and show it also inherits a construct-validity problem: general capability bleeds into safety scores in metric-dependent ways. We introduce WarrantBench, which holds the model fixed and varies the containment system, measuring four axes: containment under adversarial pressure, staging fidelity, oversight burden, and evidence reconstructability. Because the construct is a property of the system rather than the model, the capability confound is structurally excluded. We report complete confusion structures rather than headline metrics, and release tasks, transcripts and scoring under alliance governance.

Why this is legitimately a top-tier benchmark paper rather than marketing

A 2026 validity audit of agent-safety benchmarks found problems that a new benchmark can be designed to avoid by construction, and saying so is the paper's methodological contribution:

  • Metric validity. R-Judge's F1 is unsuitable because an "always unsafe" baseline attains F1=0.690, beating five discriminating models. WarrantBench reports full confusion structures and never F1 alone.
  • Construct validity. Three broad-coverage benchmarks rank the same 18 models differently, with correlation moving from −0.64 at n=7 to +0.02 at n=18 — small panels manufacture apparent patterns. WarrantBench fixes the model and varies the system, so model-panel effects do not enter.
  • Capability confound. Capability correlates ρ=+0.71 with balanced accuracy but only +0.16 with specificity. An enforcement-layer benchmark is capability-independent by construction: whether a tool allowlist holds does not depend on how clever the agent is.

That last point is the whole argument. It is not a marginal improvement on existing benchmarks; it measures a different construct, and one that is arguably what deployment decisions actually need.

P5 — Kernel-Enforced Lifetime for Unattended Agents, and the Honesty of Partial Guarantees

Target: USENIX ATC · alternate USENIX Security or a systems workshop · implementation verified

Abstract sketch. Leaving an agent running unattended requires two guarantees that pull in opposite directions: the agent must not outlive its supervisor (orphaning), and the supervisor must not die when the operator's terminal closes (terminal coupling). Satisfying one alone produces either a system you cannot leave or a process nothing can stop. We implement both — console detachment plus a kernel-enforced parent-death link — and characterise precisely what each platform can and cannot guarantee, including where the guarantee does not hold. We argue that systems making safety claims should report enforcement strength as a first-class runtime value rather than in documentation, and show a case where the honest report is "this platform cannot guarantee this".

Contributions

  1. The orphaning/terminal-coupling duality, named and shown to require joint treatment.
  2. A portable construction: Windows job objects with kill-on-close; Linux setsid plus PR_SET_PDEATHSIG; with the grandchild gap on Linux stated rather than elided.
  3. Enforcement strength as a runtime value. The system reports whether a bound is enforced or merely observed, and whether the lifetime link holds on the current platform. A limit a user believes in that does not hold is worse than no limit.
  4. Verification against real process trees rather than mocks — including that killing only the supervisor terminates the agent and its grandchildren.

Experimental plan

Existing: end-to-end verification on Windows (survives terminal exit; supervisor kill terminates agent + grandchild; deadline enforced to the second). Needed: the same matrix on Linux and macOS, adversarial escape attempts (double-fork, setsid re-parenting, process-group escape, nohup), and overhead measurement. macOS is expected to fail the parent-death property and that negative result belongs in the paper.

Rejection risk

medium-high The mechanisms are individually well known; the contribution is their composition, the duality framing, and honest partial-guarantee reporting. This may be strongest as a shorter systems paper or a well-placed workshop paper. It is also the best candidate to fold into P2 or P3 if reviewers find it thin standing alone.

P6 — Refusal Telemetry: What Agents Attempt Is a Governance Signal

Target: ACM FAccT or AIES · alternate a security workshop · data collected, unanalysed most novel, smallest

Abstract sketch. Agent platforms report what an agent did. Supervisory frameworks increasingly ask what it attempted. We observe that refusal records — deduplicated by (tool, bound) with counts — carry a signal that success logs cannot, and that the count disambiguates two very different situations conflated by a binary "denied" flag: a single refusal indicates an agent reaching beyond its grant, while twenty refusals of the same tool indicate the grant was drawn wrong. We formalise this, show it functions as a leading indicator of both misscoping and capability drift, and map it to the evidentiary expectations of principle-based regimes that require oversight to be demonstrated rather than asserted.

Why it is worth writing despite being small

It is the most clearly novel claim in the portfolio. Every agent platform logs actions; none, as far as the survey found, treats attempted-and-refused as a first-class governance artifact with the count as the discriminating variable. It is also the paper that speaks most directly to the regulatory audience — and one of the two whose framing the RFI whitepaper reuses.

Experimental plan

Needs longitudinal data the platform does not yet have: refusal streams across many runs and warrants over weeks. The analysis is cheap; the collection is the long pole, which makes this evidence-paced by definition. Candidate: instrument the OSAA benchmark runs to produce this corpus as a by-product — a good example of one artifact funding another.

The two dependencies that could stall all of this

IRB or equivalent ethics review for the human-subject work. P1 and B3 cannot run without it and the lead time is often months — start it in parallel with Stage 1, not when Stage 3 begins.

The repository still does not exist publicly. Benchmark reproducibility, artifact evaluation at security venues, and the OSS credibility every one of these papers assumes all require a public repository. It is the same blocker as publishing, and it gates the research programme too.

One caution about arXiv-first

Preprinting establishes priority on the warrant primitive and the absence/refusal distinction — real protection given how fast this area is moving. But several target venues operate anonymity periods, and a preprint can complicate double-blind submission. Check each venue's policy before posting rather than after; the security venues and the ML D&B tracks differ materially here.

Honest assessment of the portfolio

Strongest: P1, because the empirical foundation is already in the literature and the mechanism is built — it needs a study, not an invention. P4, because an enforcement-layer benchmark measures a construct nobody currently measures, and does so in a way that structurally avoids the confound a 2026 validity audit found in existing work.

Most at risk: P5, whose mechanisms are individually well known and may read as engineering rather than research; and P3, which is timely enough that someone else may publish the adjacent argument first — the nearest neighbour is already on arXiv.

What would strengthen everything: an academic collaborator with experimental capacity. Every plan above is executable solo, but the human study in particular benefits from institutional infrastructure — IRB, participant pools, and a co-author who has run one before. That was offered as an option and not taken; it remains the highest-leverage change available to this programme, and worth revisiting once the briefs are concrete enough to interest someone.
