Reaching the NeurIPS Bar

Why none of the five papers is one, what the diagnosis actually is, and six concrete concepts that could be — ranked by fit divided by cost.
Prepared 2026-08-31 · grounded in open problems the papers that beat us named in print.

The bar, stated without flattery

What NeurIPS main track actually accepts, and what we brought

NeurIPS wantsWhat the five papers have
A method, mechanism, or theoryFour measurements and a simple defense. No theory. No mechanism — every mechanism question we hit, we explicitly deferred.
Novelty that survives a related-work checkThree of five were pre-empted within days. T-03 withdrew three of its four novelty claims after a prior-art check.
Scale sufficient to convince2–6 models, 1,000–1,800 items, under $100 of compute in total.
Generality past one narrow settingOne defense type (input-side guard classifiers), one language, single-turn, one serving stack.
The honest verdict is not close. The strongest single result in the set — P8’s empty gray zone — is a domain transfer of someone else’s instrument. The most novel — P2’s input-variation defense — is a sensible technique a reviewer would call obvious in hindsight, evaluated at small scale. Neither is a NeurIPS main-track paper and no amount of rewriting makes them one.
But the diagnosis that matters is not "not novel enough." It is that we keep working on the right problems and arriving third. LongGuard beat P3 by four days. LayeredEns beat P6 by three. Certify and QualityProxy beat P8 by weeks. That is not bad luck four times — it is a structural position: we are reading the same arXiv feed as better-resourced groups and racing them at their own game, which is speed and scale. Both are the two things we do not have.

The strategic move

Stop racing on speed and scale. Compete on the thing that is actually scarce.

Across four scoops, one pattern held: the papers that beat us named their own open problems and did not solve them. Every one of them left, in writing, a gap they lacked the time or the design to close:

Paper that beat usWhat they wrote was still open
[LayeredEns]“φ is a selection statistic, not a prediction statistic… It does not predict the residual or the refusal rate of the assembled pipeline, and we demonstrate that it does not, in both directions.” Their advice is therefore “measure the assembled stack to know what you have” — i.e. there is no predictor and you must measure every stack empirically.
[LayeredEns] §11.6Its stratification is “underpowered by construction”; a larger behavior set is “the most valuable extension of this experiment.” we did this in P6
[QualityProxy] §4.4Four latent probes all failed to predict which checkpoint goes dangerous: entropy shift (p=0.606), refusal-direction geometry (cosine stays above 0.97), calibration drift (all |r|<0.09), and safety-neuron error (real at 1.39× but “global rather than regime-specific”).
[QualityProxy] §5.3It does not run a formal equivalence procedure per cell. we did this in P8
[Certify] §3.6Meeting a sample-size count “makes the test informative, it does not certify anything on its own — the test still has to be run and to pass.” we did this in P8
We have already closed three of these, and they became P6 and P8. That is the proof the strategy works — and also the proof it is being applied too timidly. We closed the small gaps. The two large ones are still open: nobody can predict a stack’s residual, and nobody can predict which model quantization will make unsafe. Those are not measurement gaps. They are prediction problems, and prediction problems are what NeurIPS publishes.
The reframe. Our scarce resource is not compute or speed — it is the discipline to run a control that kills our own result, and the per-item data to do it on. Four times now we have measured something and then measured the thing that could invalidate it. That is exactly what a prediction paper needs and a measurement paper does not, because a predictor is only worth anything if it holds out of sample, and holding out of sample is a discipline problem before it is a modeling one.

Each is stated as the claim a reviewer would have to accept, the experiments that would establish it, the compute against our $100-class ceiling, what would kill it, and how likely someone else gets there first.

1. A predictor for stack residual risk — the gap [LayeredEns] named and could not close

The claim Given pairwise measurements over candidate guards, the false-negative rate of an assembled stack is predictable within stated bounds — without assembling it. [LayeredEns] state flatly that their statistic does not do this “in both directions”, and conclude that a deployer must measure every stack empirically. That conclusion is a standing invitation.
Why this is NeurIPS-shaped and our measurements are not A measurement paper says here is what happened. This says here is an estimator, here is its error bound, here is out-of-sample validation. That is a methods contribution with theory attached, in a setting the security literature has just established as important and unsolved. It is also immediately useful: composition is how every real deployment is built, and the current advice — measure all 2n subsets — does not scale.
The experiments
  1. Fit on our existing panel. Six guards, 1,799 shared items, all 57 non-trivial subsets already measured. Fit candidate estimators — pairwise-only (Kirkwood, which P8 showed breaks past size 4), a second-order maximum-entropy model, a copula on per-item miss probabilities, and a latent-factor model where each guard loads on shared difficulty plus lineage.
  2. Hold out by subset size. Fit on pairs and triples, predict quads through the full panel. This is the honest test and the one that will hurt.
  3. Hold out by model. Leave one guard entirely out of the fit, predict every stack containing it. This tests whether the estimator generalizes to a guard it has never seen — which is what a deployer actually needs.
  4. Cross-corpus. Fit on the adversarial corpus, predict on the stratified one. P8 showed the noise floor is corpus-dependent, so an estimator that does not transfer across corpora is not a predictor.
  5. Extend the panel to 10–12 guards to widen the fit. This is the only part that costs money: roughly 6 more models × 1,799 items on A10G, about $3.
Why we are unusually well placed. The whole study is a re-analysis of per-item verdicts we already hold, plus a small panel extension. P6 cost $0.15 and produced the 57-subset dataset this needs. The marginal cost of the core result is close to zero, and our pre-registration habit is exactly the discipline a predictor paper lives or dies on.
What kills it. If no estimator beats the trivial baseline out-of-sample, there is no paper — only a stronger negative result than [LayeredEns] already published, which is a workshop note. Decide the success criterion before fitting anything, or this becomes an exercise in finding the model that flatters the data. Also: our panel is one defense type. A predictor validated only on input-side classifiers should say so in the title.
Scoop risk Moderate and rising. [LayeredEns] named the gap in print three days before we read it; naming a gap is often what a group does when it is already working on it. This has weeks, not months.
rank 2NeurIPS main tracktheory + modest compute~$20–60

2. Certified robustness for guard models under paraphrase

The claim For a given input, a guard’s verdict is provably stable under any paraphrase within a defined semantic neighborhood — with a certificate, not an empirical rate.
Why this is the natural next move Everything this program has produced about paraphrase is empirical and one-directional: we rephrase, we count flips, and the number is a lower bound because our transformation set is frozen and non-adaptive. Every one of our papers carries that caveat. A certificate removes it — it bounds all paraphrases in a neighborhood, including ones no attacker has invented. Randomized smoothing gives certified radii in continuous spaces; the open problem is doing it over a semantic neighborhood where the perturbation set is not a norm ball. That is a real methods problem, and guards are the right first target because their output is a single binary decision rather than a generation.
The experiments
  1. Define the neighborhood operationally — paraphrases whose embedding lies within ε of the original under a fixed encoder, with the encoder frozen and published. The definition is the contribution’s weakest joint and must be argued, not assumed.
  2. Smooth the guard: sample k paraphrases, majority-vote, derive a certified radius via the standard smoothing bound adapted to a discrete perturbation set.
  3. Measure the certified fraction — what share of unsafe items carry a non-trivial certificate, and at what false-positive cost. P2 already establishes the baseline this must beat and the acceptability bound it must respect.
  4. Attack the certificate. Search for a paraphrase inside the certified radius that flips the verdict. If one exists, the bound is wrong and that is the finding.
What kills it. The semantic neighborhood may not admit a sound certificate at all — embedding distance is a proxy for meaning, and our own equivalence pack exists precisely because we do not trust automated meaning judgments. If the certificate only holds under a definition nobody accepts, it is vacuous. This is the highest-risk idea here and the highest-ceiling one.
Scoop risk Low-moderate. Certified NLP robustness is an active area, but guards specifically are not the usual target.
rank 3NeurIPS main trackhigh risk~$30–80

3. Predicting which way a compressed guard breaks — where four published probes failed

The claim From the weights alone, before running a single evaluation item, predict whether quantizing a given guard will make it safety-adverse or usability-adverse.
Why this is a genuine open problem and not a fishing trip P8 established the phenomenon and could not explain it: ShieldGemma 9B loses almost only unsafe detections (144 unsafe→safe against 4), Qwen3Guard 4B almost only over-blocks, and ShieldGemma 2B reverses mid-ladder — purely usability-adverse at Q4_K_M and Q3_K_M, then 47-to-1 safety-adverse at Q2_K. Direction is a model property and nobody can predict it.

Critically, [QualityProxy] §4.4 already tried four instruments and all four failed: first-token entropy shift (p=0.606), refusal-direction geometry (cosine to the FP16 refusal direction stays above 0.97 in every quantized cell even as behavior degrades), calibration drift (all |r|<0.09), and safety-associated-neuron quantization error — real at 1.39×, p=4.89×10−7, but “global rather than regime-specific.”

Four failed probes is not a discouragement; it is a published map of where not to dig. It also means a success is unambiguous.
Why the failed probes failed, and what that suggests Every one of the four is a global, model-level statistic — an average over the network or the corpus. The phenomenon is item-level and directional: specific items flip, and which direction they flip in differs by model. A global average cannot predict a per-item directional event; that is a type mismatch, not a weak signal. The natural move is to ask what distinguishes the items that flip from the ones that do not, within a model, and then ask what distinguishes models whose flipping items are unsafe from models whose flipping items are benign.
The experiments
  1. Item-level localization. We hold the full ladder per-item for 5 guards × 7 precisions. For each flipped item, measure the margin at f16 — how close the decision was before compression. Hypothesis: flips concentrate near the boundary, and the direction is set by which side of the boundary a model’s mass sits on.
  2. The asymmetry test. If a model’s unsafe items sit closer to the boundary than its benign ones, quantization noise pushes more of them across — predicting safety-adverse. This is directly computable from the f16 logits and needs no new runs beyond re-scoring with logit capture.
  3. Causal intervention. Patch f16 activations into the quantized model at each layer and find where the verdict is restored. This localizes where the damage happens, which is the mechanism claim.
  4. Out-of-sample prediction. Fit the margin-asymmetry predictor on 4 guards, predict direction for a held-out guard before running its ladder. Pre-register the prediction. This is the whole paper — everything before it is setup.
What kills it. If margin asymmetry does not predict direction, we have a fifth failed probe, and a fifth failed probe is not publishable at NeurIPS. Set the kill criterion before looking: if held-out direction prediction is no better than the base rate, stop and write it up as a short note that saves the next group the attempt.
Compute Logit capture across the existing ladder is a re-run with a different output hook — the ladder itself is 35 cells at roughly P8’s cost. Activation patching on 0.6B–4B runs on the local card or Kaggle’s free tier. The binding constraint is engineering time, not money.
rank 4NeurIPS D&Bhighest effort~$150–400

4. The first guard benchmark that reports what its own numbers cannot distinguish

The claim Every existing guard benchmark reports differences smaller than its own noise floor and none measures that floor. This one ships with it.
Why this is the most durable idea here, and the most expensive It is the one place where our accumulated methodology is the contribution rather than a credibility wrapper around someone else’s. Four findings, each already established in our own work, become design requirements no existing benchmark meets:
  • A measured per-corpus noise floor (P1, and P8’s finding that it is corpus-dependent: 0.09% stratified against 1.0–5.7% on verdict-selected sets).
  • Mandatory per-item release, because aggregate rates conceal per-item churn — [Certify] measured that at roughly 5× on benchmark accuracy, and P8 at 1.86× on guard verdicts.
  • Directional scoring, because unsafe→safe and safe→unsafe are not the same event and a single churn number describes both equally badly (P8 §5.2).
  • A declared equivalence margin, because [Certify]’s audit found none of 16 eligible published claims declares one.
No current benchmark does any of the four. A benchmark that does all four, at scale, with adoption, is a D&B paper and a durable citation base.
What kills it. Scale and adoption. A benchmark nobody runs is a dataset nobody cites, and we cannot fund the scale that drives adoption — multilingual, multi-turn, many models, a leaderboard someone maintains. This is the idea to pursue with a collaborator who has compute, not alone. Treat it as an 18-month play, not a next-cycle submission.

5. An adaptive attacker, and a defense that survives it

The claim P2’s input-variation defense holds against an attacker who optimizes against the defense itself — not merely against a frozen transformation set.
Why this is the single highest-value upgrade to existing work Every paper in this program carries the same caveat, stated as a strength: the transformation set is frozen and non-adaptive, so every effect is a lower bound. That is honest, and it is also the exact limitation [LayeredEns] show is decisive. They ran two adaptive adversaries and found a defense’s measured worth changes completely with the attack class — a perplexity filter blocks 100/100 optimized suffixes and 0/100 fluent prompts, so “a composition estimate assembled from per-defense figures obtained under different attacks is not merely imprecise but can be optimistic without bound.”

P2 currently claims a defense works against an attacker who is not trying. A defense that survives adaptive attack is a categorically stronger claim, and guards are the right target because they are classifiers — white-box gradient attacks apply directly, unlike attacks on a generation.
The experiments
  1. Build the adaptive attacker. Gradient-based search over paraphrase space against the smoothed guard, not the bare one — the attacker must know the defense. Open weights make this available for all six panel guards.
  2. Re-measure P2’s defense under it, against the same pre-fixed false-positive budget. The budget must not move; that is what made P2 credible.
  3. Attack-class matrix. Report each defense against each attack class, never a single number — the precise error [LayeredEns] warn produces unbounded optimism.
  4. Transfer. Do adaptive attacks built against one guard transfer to another? Our M1 panel already shows non-adaptive evasions transfer at 2.5–6× base rate; whether adaptive ones transfer more or less is open and interesting either way.
What kills it. The defense may simply not survive — which is publishable, but as a negative result at a security venue rather than a NeurIPS method. Also: optimization loops are the most compute-hungry idea here, and a weak attacker producing a flattering result is worse than no experiment. Budget the attacker generously or do not run it.
rank 6workshop → main track~$40–100

6. Scaling laws for guard fragility — and the one axis where scale does not help

The claim Guard fragility is predictable from scale on some perturbation axes and provably not on others, and the exception is the one that matters most operationally.
The result we already have that motivates it T-03 §5.12 records that the 0.6B is less stable than the 4B under four independent perturbations — rephrasing, evasion, quantization, and adversarial search — “four unrelated perturbations, one ordering.” Then P3-X found a fifth that breaks it: under long-input dilution, 19 of 60 payloads degrade and 0 improve at both scales, identically.

That is a genuinely interesting asymmetry, and we currently report it in a single paragraph of a workshop note. The claim it supports — scale buys robustness to perturbations of the model’s judgment, and buys nothing against perturbations of the input’s structure — is a clean, testable, general statement. If it holds across families and a real scale range, it is a paper.
The experiments
  1. Widen the scale ladder within families: Qwen3Guard already spans 0.6B/4B/8B; add every open guard family with three or more sizes.
  2. Run all five perturbation axes on every point — the harnesses exist and are pre-registered.
  3. Fit the scaling relation per axis and test whether the structural axes (length, position) genuinely have zero slope while the judgment axes have negative slope.
  4. Predict. Hold out a family, predict its fragility from its sizes. Same discipline as concept 1 — a scaling law that does not predict out of family is a curve fit.
What kills it. Too few open guard families with enough sizes. Check that inventory before committing; if there are only two usable families, this is a workshop note about two families and should be built that way from the start.

What to stop doing

Stop replicating. P3, P6 and P8 are each a careful replication of work published days earlier. They are honest and they are worth submitting to the workshops they now target — but three in one week is a pattern, not a coincidence, and the pattern is that we are reading a feed and reacting to it. Reacting is structurally second place.
Stop treating pre-registration as the contribution. It is a credibility asset and it makes our numbers trustworthy, which matters. It is not novelty, and no reviewer accepts a paper because the hypotheses were hashed. Use it as the thing that makes a prediction claim believable, which is the one place it converts into scientific weight.
Keep doing the one thing that is genuinely rare. Four times in this program we measured something and then ran the control that could destroy it — the borderline floor against P8’s effect, the significance test against P3-X’s non-monotonicity, the pooled ratio against P8’s denominator, φmax against P6’s family contrast. Two of those four killed our own claim. That habit is worth more in a prediction paper than in a measurement paper, because a predictor that has survived its author’s attempts to break it is the only kind anyone should believe.

The single sentence version. We have been writing careful papers about what happens, in a field where three better-resourced groups write the same papers faster. The way out is not to measure more things. It is to predict one thing nobody can currently predict — and two of those are sitting in print, named as open, with our data already collected against them.
