The Non-Developer Platform

Can Warrantor serve the people who oversee agents and the people who want to use them — without a terminal? Yes. This is the feature, functionality and workflow catalogue, grounded in what exists and in what the markets we sell into are actually asking for.

aumos @ integration/end-to-end · 11 August 2026 · research-backed design, measured against source

The short answer is yes, and the starting position is more favourable than it looks. The hard part of a governance console — deciding what a non-technical person needs to see, and what they are allowed to decide — is largely done and sitting in the codebase. What is missing is the rendering.

The honest starting position: the non-developer surface is 0% built

Before any design, the measurement. There is no user interface anywhere in this repository — no .tsx, no next.config, no .jsx. Every surface is a CLI, an SDK, an MCP server, or a library.

@warrantor/console is a headless TypeScript library: data model, view reducers, API client, 263 lines, 12 tests. Its own docstring says "The React/Next.js component layer is task 03" — never built. The console service in docker-compose.yml publishes port 3000 and serves a placeholder page that says so in its health response (mode: "placeholder (X7 library built)"). It is labelled, so it is not deceptive; it is simply not a product yet. arena is in the same state.

This matters for planning: the work below is genuinely greenfield UI, not a refactor. It is also why the estimate is smaller than it sounds — the data model, the state reducers, the approval semantics, and the API client already exist and are tested.

What the markets we sell into are actually asking for

The design below is not derived from what a console usually contains. It is derived from what supervisors in our three lead markets — US/North America, GCC, and India — have said organisations must be able to produce. Those requirements turn out to name a feature list almost directly.

United States — there is no specification, and that is the argument

On 17 April 2026 the OCC, Federal Reserve and FDIC issued revised interagency model risk management guidance (OCC Bulletin 2026-13, Fed SR 26-2), superseding SR 11-7 and SR 21-8. It says plainly that generative AI and agentic AI models are novel and rapidly evolving, and as such are not within the scope of this guidance.

The tempting reading is that agents are therefore unregulated. That is wrong, and getting it wrong is expensive. What was withdrawn is the framework that would have specified the controls. Every obligation attached to the underlying action — safety and soundness, consumer protection, sectoral duties, third-party risk — is untouched. The guidance is explicit about the consequence: an organisation's own "risk management and governance practices should guide the determination of appropriate governance and controls for any tools, processes, or systems not covered in this document."

The part that changes the roadmap

The agencies have stated they plan to issue a request for information addressing model risk management generally and specifically banks' use of AI including generative and agentic AI.

An RFI is a comment window. The eventual specification will be written against what the industry can already demonstrate. An organisation that can produce a complete, signed record of how its agents were bounded and who approved what is not merely compliant early — it is in the room while the rule is drafted. That is a materially stronger reason to build the evidence layer now than "a rule is coming."

GCC — the requirement is written almost as a feature list

Saudi Arabia designated 2026 the Year of Artificial Intelligence, and on 14 July 2026 SDAIA launched a national AI risk management framework giving government and private-sector entities a unified methodology for identifying, assessing, treating and continuously monitoring AI risks. It sits alongside the PDPL, SDAIA's AI Ethics Principles, and an AI Adoption Framework with four maturity levels.

In the UAE, DIFC Regulation 10 specifically regulates autonomous and semi-autonomous systems, requiring privacy impact assessments and risk-based audits for automated decision-making — the most specific AI governance provision in the Gulf. And on 23 April 2026 the UAE announced plans to move 50% of federal government sectors, services and operations to agentic AI within two years.

The guidance to organisations is unusually concrete. They are told to prepare AI inventories, use-case ownership, testing controls, risk classification, monitoring processes, and evidence of accountability. Read that list again as a product backlog:

The word doing the most work is continuously. A point-in-time attestation is what most governance tooling produces. An agent that ran unattended at 3am and was refused eleven times is not a point-in-time fact, and that gap is where this product's evidence is differentiated rather than merely present.

India — a principle-based framework, which needs artefacts to be met with

The RBI's FREE-AI framework (Framework for Responsible and Ethical Enablement of AI in the Financial Sector) sets out seven guiding sutras with practical guidance for institutions developing or deploying AI. The India AI Governance Guidelines take the same principle-based, techno-legal approach, and DPDP governs the personal data an agent touches while working.

A principle-based regime is harder to satisfy than a checklist, not easier: it asks an institution to demonstrate that a principle held, which is an evidentiary claim. "Human oversight was maintained" is a sentence; "here are 340 consequential actions, each of which a named person approved before it committed, with signatures" is proof of it.

A gap in our own materials, found while researching this — since fixed

When first written, the platform's compliance matrix tracked ten frameworks of which four were EU, and RBI/FREE-AI, DPDP, SDAIA and DIFC Regulation 10 did not appear at all — backwards relative to where we sell.

The matrix (docs/cross-cutting/13-compliance-frameworks.md) has been re-cut: US interagency MRM (OCC 2026-13 / Fed SR 26-2, with the deferral-not-exemption reading), RBI FREE-AI, the India AI Governance Guidelines and DPDP, SDAIA's risk framework and DIFC Reg 10 now lead, with an evidence table mapping what each supervisor asks for to the primitive that produces it. EU frameworks remain tracked where factually required. GCC entries carry an explicit primary-text-confirmation caveat.

Two of these want to operate; five want to govern. The catalogue is organised accordingly, because a single "dashboard" that tries to do both is how governance tools end up unused by operators and untrusted by auditors.

Family A — Operate: running an agent without a terminal

The central design move, and the reason this is tractable at all:

Non-developers never author bounds. They launch pre-bounded work.

Asking an operations manager to specify a tool allowlist, write-path globs and egress hosts is asking them to do security engineering. They will either refuse or approve whatever is suggested, and the second is worse.

So an engineer authors a template once — a frozen WarrantBounds shape with a goal, a deadline and a set of parameters — and anyone may launch it. The warrant is not a form the operator fills in; it is the thing the template is. This keeps the security decision with the person qualified to make it while putting the capability in everyone's hands.

A1 — Job templates new

A named, reusable piece of pre-scoped work: Update dependency versions, Triage new bug reports, Draft the release notes, Reconcile last month's invoices. Each template is a `WarrantBounds` with declared parameters (a repo, a date range, a ticket ID) and a fixed everything-else.

Builds on: WarrantBounds, Warrant::grant. New: a template store, parameter substitution, and a review step so a template change is itself an approved event — a template is standing authority, and editing one silently would be a privilege escalation.

A2 — Plain-language scope preview half exists

Before anything runs, in prose, both halves:

Update dependency versions — what this can and cannot do
It will be able to: read this repository · edit files under src/ and Cargo.toml · run cargo and git · reach crates.io · open one pull request staged — you approve it first
It cannot: reach any other site · send email or messages · spend money · delete anything · touch your working copy it works in an isolated checkout
It stops: after 4 hours, automatically enforced by the operating system

The "cannot" list is not a promise written by a marketer — it is derivable, because an absent bound in this system means none rather than unlimited. Empty egress hosts means no network. That design decision is what makes an honest negative statement possible at all, and the negative half is the half a non-technical person actually needs.

Builds on: WarrantBounds, bound_strengths(). New: the prose renderer.

A3 — Enforcement-strength badges already in code

Every bound is shown with whether it is enforced (the system refuses the action) or observed (the system measures and reports, but cannot prevent). BoundStrength already exists in the Rust crate and already marks budget as the one Observed bound — because model API calls do not pass through us, so a spend ceiling is a measurement, not a wall.

Why this is the single most important honesty feature

A non-technical user reading "budget: $50" will believe $50 is a limit. If it is really a measurement, that belief is a liability we manufactured. Showing enforced and measured only as distinct, unmissable states is the difference between a governance tool and a comfort blanket.

It is also a competitive asset that costs nothing to ship: most tooling presents all its controls with equal visual confidence.

A4 — Live run view state exists

What is running, started when, how long remains, what it has staged so far, what it has been refused. Not a log tail — a status a manager can read.

Builds on: warrantor status, DaemonState::reconcile, Reconciliation. New: streaming, and a run-timeline rendering.

A5 — The morning review core exists

The workflow the whole product exists to make possible. One screen, three questions.

Overnight run — “fix the flaky auth test” ran 6h 12m, finished 04:41
1. What changed — 3 files in the authentication module. Added a retry when the token refresh fails. summary · view the exact changes
2. What it wants to do — nothing has happened yet:
  ① open a pull request “Retry token refresh on 401”
  ② post a comment explaining the change attaches to ①
  ③ add the label security attaches to ①
3. What it was refused — asked to run curl 11 times outside its allowed tools. Eleven attempts suggests the bounds are wrong, not that the agent misbehaved.
Decide: Approve all three   Approve some   Discard everything

Builds on: warrantor report, StagingQueue::release_order(), Proxy::authority_requests(), settle() / void(). New: the change summariser and the layout.

Where this feature can hurt someone, and the mitigation

"What changed", rendered in prose for a non-coder, is a summary — and a summary of a code change can be wrong or incomplete in ways the reader cannot detect. Someone approving a database migration on the strength of a sentence that omitted the destructive part is a failure mode we would have introduced.

So: summaries are labelled as summaries, the exact change is always one click away and never collapsed behind a paywall of clicks, and any staged effect in the destructive or financial class refuses to render a prose-only approval path. For those, the reviewer sees the literal operation. Convenience is not extended to the classes where being wrong is unrecoverable.

A6 — Dependency-aware partial approval semantics exist

Staged effects release in topological order, so a reviewer cannot approve the comment while rejecting the pull request it attaches to. The interface should express that as a structural fact — greying the dependent, not erroring after the fact.

Builds on: release_order(), which already computes it.

A7 — "It stopped halfway" explained semantics exist

When a settle fails partway, the warrant goes to Held, not Settled, and stops at the boundary rather than compensating. For a non-developer that needs saying plainly:

3 of 5 completed. The pull request is real and open. It stopped at the comment because GitHub rejected it. Nothing after that point was attempted. The remaining two are still waiting on you.

"Stop and report" rather than "roll back" is the right behaviour — you cannot un-send an email — but it is only safe if the person reading understands exactly where the line fell.

Family B — Govern: overseeing agents other people run

B1 — Agent & authority register data exists

The inventory supervisors ask for, and the answer to "what can our agents currently do?" — every warrant, its owner, its bounds, its state, its history. Live, not a quarterly spreadsheet.

Builds on: WarrantStore::list(). New: an owner field, and a register view.

B2 — Risk classification exists

SideEffectClass already partitions actions into read / write / financial / destructive / physical. For a compliance officer that becomes a single readable statement: "Three agents can spend money. None can delete production data. Fourteen can only read." Policy then attaches to the class rather than to individual tools, which is the level a non-engineer can actually reason about.

B3 — Approval policy & segregation of duties primitive exists

Which classes require human approval, and who may give it. The underlying separation is already structural rather than configured: the agent holds an act-scoped capability token with no settle field, and the settle key is separate. The console turns that into a role a compliance officer can administer — including the rule that the person who launched a run may not be the person who approves it.

Builds on: settle-key separation, warrantor-rbac, the console's existing ApprovalRequest model.

B4 — The refused-request report exists, and is genuinely differentiated

Every wall an agent hit, deduplicated with counts. Most platforms can tell you what an agent did. This tells you what it tried and was stopped from doing — which is the more interesting question for both security and scoping.

And the count is the signal. One refusal of a tool is an agent reaching for something it should not have. Twenty refusals of the same tool is a bound that was drawn wrong, and a person wasting their morning. The same data answers a security question and a usability one.

Builds on: AuthorityRequest { tool, bound, reason, count }, already deduplicated and sorted by frequency.

B5 — Evidence pack export plumbing exists

One action produces everything an auditor needs for a period: warrants granted with their bounds, receipts, approval decisions with approvers, refusals, and the settle/void outcome — signed, hash-chained, and exportable.

Builds on: OCSF 1.9.0 export (class_uid 6003), the append-only hash-chained queue, warrantor-retention. New: period selection, packaging, and a rendered cover sheet.

B6 — Continuous monitoring & drift new

SDAIA's framework asks for continuous monitoring, not an annual attestation. The question that matters: are agents asking for more than they used to? A steady rise in refusals against one bound, or in the number of warrants requesting a financial class, is the leading indicator — visible well before an incident.

Builds on: the reconciliation timer and refusal history. New: the trend model.

B7 — Framework mapping, with the gaps shown matrix exists, wrong markets

Map collected evidence to NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, RBI FREE-AI's sutras, SDAIA's risk framework and DIFC Regulation 10 — and show what is not covered as prominently as what is. A compliance view that only shows green is a liability, because someone will rely on it.

Unblocked: the matrix now carries the India and GCC frameworks with an evidence mapping, so this view is a rendering task rather than a research task.

B8 — Stop everything mechanism exists

One control, reachable by someone who has never used a terminal, that halts every running agent — with a confirmation naming exactly what will be killed, and a receipt afterwards recording who stopped it and why.

Builds on: kill-switch, Supervisor::terminate(), terminate_group(), which already kill the whole process tree including grandchildren.

B9 — Spend visibility, labelled honestly measured only

What agent activity cost. Shown with the measured only badge throughout, because model API calls do not traverse our proxy and so the figure is an observation rather than a ceiling. Presenting it as a limit would be the exact failure A3 exists to prevent.

B10 — Pre-authorisation review for procurement new

Before a new agent or template is permitted, a reviewer sees its proposed scope in the same plain language as A2 and approves the bounds, not a questionnaire. Vendor risk assessment becomes a live artefact that stays true, rather than a PDF that was accurate on the day it was signed.

One decision to make before Phase 0

The read API needs an owner. Today the warrant store is a directory of JSON files under ~/.warrantor on one developer's machine — correct for a CLI, insufficient for a console that several people read and two of them approve from. Multi-user means a service, which means identity, which is where agent-identity and the SPIFFE work already in the repo become load-bearing rather than adjacent.

That is the real architectural fork, and it is worth deciding deliberately rather than discovering halfway through Phase 1.

Answering the question directly

Can we add a comprehensive non-developer surface? Yes — and the reason it is tractable is that the difficult decisions are already made and already in the code. Warrantor is unusually well positioned for this because the primitives a governance console needs are not bolt-ons here; they are the design:

  • Staged effects give a non-developer something to approve that has not happened yet. Without them, "human oversight" is a person being told about something after it committed.
  • Absent bound = none makes an honest "it cannot do X" list derivable rather than aspirational.
  • Enforced vs Observed already exists, so the console can be truthful about which controls are walls and which are measurements.
  • Settle-key separation makes segregation of duties structural, so a compliance officer is administering a real boundary rather than a UI convention.
  • Refusal records with counts produce a governance artefact most platforms cannot: what was attempted and stopped.

The two things standing between here and there are honest and unglamorous: there is no UI at all, and there is no service to read from. Neither is a research problem.
